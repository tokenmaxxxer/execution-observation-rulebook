# QA Practice Landscape Research

This is a point-in-time research report, gathered to ground the design of a QA cycle state machine. It compiles findings from four independent research passes, each covering a distinct axis of QA practice: methodology lineages, the judgment values of the QA role, human gates in a real QA workflow, and the QA role under automation and AI. Each section was produced independently and is reproduced verbatim below, in the order it was gathered. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is recorded as contested rather than artificially resolved — this report does not adjudicate between competing schools of practice. The "Implies for the QA cycle" subsections in each part are candidate inputs (states, transitions, human decision points) for later state-machine design, not a finished design themselves.

## Methodology lineages

### Practice

**Formal/scripted process (ISTQB / IEEE 829 / V-model lineage).** The ISTQB Certified Tester Foundation Level Syllabus (v4.0.1, istqb.org) formalizes a test process of planning, monitoring and control, analysis, design, implementation, execution, and completion, with test design producing test conditions and test case specifications and test implementation producing prioritized, organized test procedure specifications — terminology drawn directly from IEEE Std 829-1998, "Standard for Software Test Documentation" (standards.ieee.org/ieee/829/3787). IEEE 829 defines a fixed document set — test plan, test design specification, test case specification, test procedure specification, test log, test incident report, test summary report — each with a prescribed structure so that test work is auditable independent of who performed it. In V-model practice each development level (requirements, architecture, design, unit) has a mirrored verification level planned before coding starts: test planning and test-basis analysis begin as soon as the corresponding development artifact (requirements spec, design spec) is baselined, i.e., testing starts in parallel with, not after, development, but test *execution* waits until code exists. IEEE 829-1998 has since been superseded by the ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119 family, which standardizes test processes, document templates, and design techniques (per the same ISTQB material and Wikipedia's "Software test documentation"). Consumers of these documents are typically project management, QA leads, auditors, and downstream test teams who did not write the tests themselves — the documentation is the handoff mechanism. "Done" is defined by exit criteria stated in the test plan (e.g., coverage percentage, defect density thresholds, all planned test cases executed) — a condition checkable from the documents themselves, not from tester judgment in the moment.

**Exploratory testing / context-driven school (Kaner, Bach, Bolton).** In 1999 Cem Kaner, James Bach and colleagues declared themselves a distinct "school" of testing thought, Context-Driven Testing (per rapid-software-testing.com's author bios and multiple secondary sources). Bach popularized exploratory testing in the 1990s; with Michael Bolton he later formalized it as Rapid Software Testing (RST) — "a methodology and mindset for testing software expertly and credibly in uncertain conditions and under extreme time pressure" (rapid-software-testing.com). RST explicitly fuses test design, execution, result interpretation, and learning into one continuous act rather than separate phases — the tester learns about the product *while* testing it, so test starts the moment there is anything observable (a spec, a mockup, a running build), not on a document schedule. There is no fixed artifact set: the record of testing is the tester's notes, and the tester is expected to justify their testing on demand rather than have pre-written scripts stand in for that justification. "Done" is a judgment call — testing stops when the risk of continuing is judged lower than the cost, made explicit and defensible by the tester, not a documentable percentage.

**Session-based test management (SBTM — Jonathan and James Bach).** Developed in the early 2000s specifically to give exploratory testing the structure and management oversight that formal-process critics said it lacked (virtuosoqa.com; satisfice.com "Session-Based Test Management"). The unit of work is a *session*: an "uninterrupted, chartered block of reviewable test effort," per Jonathan Bach. A *charter* is a short mission statement written before the session — a goal or agenda, editable mid-flight — created by the test team, not a full script. During the session the tester keeps a *session sheet*: the charter plus areas for time breakdown (setup, test design/execution, bug investigation), notes, issues, and questions found. Sessions are time-boxed (commonly ~60–120 minutes) so throughput and coverage become countable even though the moment-to-moment path is unscripted. After the session, a *debrief* between tester and lead uses the PROOF structure — Past, Results, Obstacles, Outlook, Feelings — to review what happened and coach the tester (onesoftwaretester.wordpress.com summarizing Jonathan Bach). Session sheets are consumed by test leads for coverage/status rollups (a lightweight metrics layer bolted onto an otherwise scriptless method), which is precisely how SBTM answers the "how do you manage something unscripted" objection from the formal camp.

**Risk-based testing.** Per the ISTQB glossary and syllabus (istqb-glossary.page; istqb.org), risk-based testing uses product-risk identification and risk levels — likelihood × impact — to guide test planning, design, and execution from the earliest stages of a project. The common practice is depth-first risk ordering: highest-risk areas are tested first and most thoroughly, lower-risk areas get shallower coverage or are deferred/skipped under time pressure (tryqa.com; cania-consulting.com). This is less a standalone lineage than a *prioritization layer* that can sit on top of either the formal or the exploratory camp — ISTQB folds it into scripted planning (risk tables drive which IEEE-829-style test cases get written first), while context-driven practitioners fold it into charter selection (which areas get session time first). "Waste" in this lineage is testing effort spent on low-risk, low-impact areas at the expense of high-risk ones — it explicitly reframes exhaustive coverage itself as potentially wasteful if it's not risk-weighted.

### Contested

The central fault line is whether IEEE-829-style documentation is *evidence of quality work* or *overhead that substitutes for actual thinking*. The context-driven school (Kaner, Bach, Bolton) rejects the formal school's insistence on pre-scripted test cases and heavyweight documentation as its defining position: RST treats detailed test scripts as a poor proxy for tester skill, arguing scripts written before anyone has seen the running product bake in ignorance and cannot adapt when reality diverges from the spec, while producing an audit trail that looks rigorous without correlating well with bugs actually found. The formal/ISTQB-IEEE 829 lineage counters (implicitly, in its own documentation-centric syllabus and in the ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119 standardization effort) that traceability, repeatability, and independent auditability require artifacts that don't depend on any one tester's tacit skill — an organization can't inspect, regulate, or hand off a purely tacit process. Neither source set in this research resolves the disagreement; it is presented here as live. SBTM itself sits in contested territory: it was built by context-driven practitioners as a concession to management's need for oversight, which some in the pure exploratory camp see as unnecessary bureaucracy and some in the formal camp see as still too loose (a "charter" is not a test case, a session sheet is not a test log in the IEEE 829 sense — there's no expected-result-per-input record). Risk-based testing is contested on a different axis: how risk levels are actually assessed (quantitative scoring vs. expert/team consensus) and by whom (testers alone vs. cross-functional stakeholders) — the arxiv survey "Recent Results on Classifying Risk-Based Testing Approaches" flags that the field has many divergent risk-based testing approaches without a settled classification, i.e., disagreement about method, not just application.

### Implies for the QA cycle

- **Candidate STATES:** `test-planned` (risk-based/formal), `charter-drafted` (SBTM), `session-in-progress` / `session-timeboxed`, `test-designed`, `test-implemented`, `test-executed`, `debriefed`, `test-summary-published`, `risk-assessed`, `exit-criteria-met`.
- **Candidate TRANSITIONS:** requirements-baselined → test-basis-analyzed (formal/V-model, starts parallel to dev); charter-written → session-started → session-timeboxed-end → debrief-held → charter-revised-or-closed (SBTM); risk-identified → risk-scored → coverage-depth-assigned → test-order-sequenced (risk-based, can feed either formal test-case generation or exploratory charter selection); test-case-specified → test-procedure-organized → test-executed → incident-logged → summary-reported (IEEE 829/ISTQB); exploration → learning → design-adjustment (looped, RST — deliberately not phase-gated).
- **Candidate HUMAN DECISION POINTS:** setting exit criteria / "done" thresholds in a test plan (formal); tester's real-time judgment call to stop or redirect exploration mid-session (context-driven — explicitly not automatable per RST's own framing); test lead's coaching feedback during PROOF debrief; assigning/adjusting risk likelihood-and-impact scores (who scores, and whether testers alone vs. cross-functional review); choosing which lineage governs a given feature/release (documentation-heavy vs. charter-based) — itself a risk/context judgment call not dictated by any single standard.

## Judgment values of the QA role

### Practice

The defect-vs-intended-behavior boundary is not resolved by a formal test — it is resolved by argument. Cem Kaner's teaching on the **oracle problem** holds that oracles (the rules a tester uses to decide pass/fail) are "necessarily incomplete," so no checklist can mechanically settle whether an observed behavior is a bug; a human has to judge it against multiple, sometimes conflicting, oracles (Kaner, "The Oracle Problem and the Teaching of Software Testing," https://kaner.com/?p=190). Kaner's companion concept, **bug advocacy**, treats the report itself as a persuasion artifact: "we are advocating for a fix," which means the tester's job includes building a case — with evidence and impact — for why a given behavior should be classified and treated as a defect rather than dismissed as intended (Kaner, "Bug Advocacy," https://kaner.com/pdfs/BugAdvocacy.pdf; see also Kenst's practitioner summary, https://www.kenst.com/how-to-write-a-good-bug-report-and-be-a-bug-advocate/). In practice this dispute is closed procedurally, not philosophically: engineering leads or triage owners make the final call and record it as a resolution (WORKSFORME, INVALID, WONTFIX) rather than as a truth claim.

Severity and priority are treated as two independent axes with different owners. Bugzilla's own documentation defines **severity** as "how severe the problem is" — from blocker to trivial — an assessment of technical/functional impact, while **priority** (Bugzilla's P1–P5) describes "the importance and order in which a bug should be fixed compared to other bugs" (Bugzilla 5.2+ docs, "Understanding a Bug," https://bugzilla.readthedocs.io/en/latest/using/understanding.html). Convention across trackers (Bugzilla, and mirrored in Jira's severity/priority fields) is that severity is typically set or proposed by the reporter/QA based on observed impact, while priority is set by engineering/release management balancing severity against schedule, business context, and workaround availability — the same S1 bug can be P1 or P4 depending on shipping constraints (Eclipse WTP conventions, https://wiki.eclipse.org/WTP/Conventions_of_bug_priority_and_severity). Mozilla's Firefox source docs make the severity ladder concrete: S1/Critical ("crashes with data loss," core feature broken for all users), S2/Major (impact serious enough users might switch products, or QA-flagged), S3/Minor (narrow-configuration or low-frequency issues), with new bugs defaulting to unset severity (`--`) until triaged (Mozilla, "Defect Severity," https://firefox-source-docs.mozilla.org/bug-mgmt/guides/severity.html). Microsoft's Security Development Lifecycle (SDL) "bug bar" is the sharpest published example of severity being pre-defined and non-negotiable at the rating level — it fixes severity thresholds (e.g., Elevation of Privilege can never be rated below "Important" regardless of user-interaction mitigations) precisely so severity judgment isn't re-litigated bug by bug (Microsoft, "SDL Security Bug Bar," https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/msrc/sdlbugbar; sample at https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/security/engineering/security-bug-bar-sample).

Reproducibility is the practical gate for whether a report gets acted on at all. Mozilla's published triage policy states that a bug lacking steps to reproduce should be "needinfo'd" (a formal request-for-information flag against the reporter), and that triagers "are not obligated to wait forever for a response" — unanswered needinfo requests lead to the report being resolved **INCOMPLETE** (Mozilla, "Triage for Bugzilla," https://firefox-source-docs.mozilla.org/bug-mgmt/policies/triage-bugzilla.html). A distinct resolution, **WORKSFORME** (Bugzilla's canonical term, mirrored elsewhere as "cannot reproduce"), is reserved for cases where the triager actively tried to reproduce and failed; Mozilla's own guidance to triagers cautions this should be applied carefully, since the failure to reproduce may reflect a platform or environment mismatch rather than the bug being false — matching the same OS/build as the reporter is called out explicitly as a precondition before closing this way. **WONTFIX** is used where the behavior is acknowledged as a real defect but deliberately not going to be addressed (e.g., low severity on an unsupported branch), and is kept conceptually separate from "not a bug" resolutions like INVALID/WORKSFORME. Academic work on reproduction-step quality (Zamith Alves et al., "Assessing the Quality of the Steps to Reproduce in Bug Reports," ACM ESEC/FSE 2019, https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07107) documents this as a known and costly practice problem: "the (lack of high) quality of the reported steps to reproduce the bugs... leads to excessive manual effort spent on bug triage and resolution," and catalogs the recurring failure modes — ambiguous steps, vocabulary mismatch between reporter and product terminology, and outright missing steps.

Triage itself, per Mozilla's published policy, is a defined discipline, not an ad hoc meeting: engineering managers/directors name Triage Owners per component; new bugs get a same-day pass for security/urgent issues, a weekly team meeting handles bugs needing discussion, and every new bug is expected to be fully triaged or under active investigation within one week. What triage decides is explicit and bounded: severity, priority, correct component routing, and bug type (defect vs. task vs. enhancement) — it does not re-derive the fix (Mozilla, triage-bugzilla.html, cited above).

On report anatomy, both QA practitioner literature and tracker conventions converge on the same skeleton: a descriptive **title** (to catch duplicates and let a scanning triager understand impact without opening the report), numbered **steps to reproduce** starting from a known state, explicit **expected vs. actual** behavior, **environment** (build/OS/browser/device), and **evidence** (screenshot, log, video). Industry summaries synthesizing this convention describe it as: "a solid bug report needs five core components: a descriptive title, reproduction steps, expected and actual behavior, environment details, and evidence" (QAwerk/industry synthesis, https://qawerk.com/blog/how-to-write-a-good-bug-report-best-practices/; similar in TestDevLab, https://www.testdevlab.com/blog/how-to-write-a-good-bug-report). Kaner's bug-advocacy framing adds a normative layer on top of that skeleton: a good report is not just complete, it is *persuasive and non-authoritative in tone* — presenting evidence and a credible case for severity/impact rather than issuing a verdict, since the author's job is advocacy, not adjudication (Kaner, Bug Advocacy PDF, cited above).

### Contested

- **Whether severity should ever be reporter-set at all.** Some shops let the filer set initial severity as a good-faith estimate (Bugzilla's default workflow, Mozilla's S1–S4 self-assignment on file); others treat severity as something only a triager/engineer can properly assign, on the theory that reporters systematically overrate their own bugs. Neither convention is universal across the sources found.
- **How strict the "must reproduce to be actionable" bar should be.** Mozilla's own guidance flags internal tension: closing as WORKSFORME risks wrongly dismissing a real, environment-dependent bug, but leaving non-reproducible reports open indefinitely clogs the backlog — the policy documents caution on both sides without resolving which failure mode is worse, and different components under the same tracker apply different patience thresholds before INCOMPLETE.
- **Priority as engineering-owned vs. jointly negotiated.** Tracker documentation (Bugzilla, Eclipse conventions) frames priority as something programmers/release managers set, but practitioner blogs on severity-vs-priority (BrowserStack, Bird Eats Bug, QA Madness — industry sources, not standards bodies) describe QA leads and product owners routinely negotiating priority in triage meetings, which is a materially different ownership model than "engineering decides."
- **Is "works as designed" a legitimate defect-boundary resolution or a dodge?** Mozilla's internal guidance itself acknowledges the social risk — advising triagers to soften the WORKSFORME/INVALID message because "the bug reporter may get offended" — which is itself evidence the practitioner community does not treat this resolution as a clean, uncontested closure; it is a known friction point between QA and engineering, not a settled procedure.
- **Whether bug advocacy (persuasive framing) is a virtue or a bias risk.** Kaner's model asks testers to actively argue for their bugs; critics in the broader testing community note this can shade into advocacy overriding neutral severity assessment, especially under reporter incentive structures (bug counts, SLAs).

### Implies for the QA cycle

- **STATES**: `Reported` (unclassified) → `Needs-Info` (reproduction/detail requested) → `Triaged` (severity + priority + component assigned) → `Confirmed-Reproduced` / `Could-Not-Reproduce` → `Resolved: Fixed | WontFix | WorksForMe | Invalid | Duplicate | Incomplete`
- **TRANSITIONS**:
  - `Reported → Needs-Info` when steps/environment/evidence are missing or ambiguous (matches Mozilla's needinfo mechanism and the ESEC/FSE paper's "missing/ambiguous steps" taxonomy)
  - `Needs-Info → Incomplete` after a bounded silence window (Mozilla: no fixed universal timeout, but "not obligated to wait forever")
  - `Reported/Needs-Info → Triaged` gated on a triage-owner decision, not automatic
  - `Triaged → Could-Not-Reproduce → WorksForMe` only after an actual reproduction attempt on matching environment/build, not on inspection alone
  - `Triaged → Invalid/WontFix` when the "is this a bug" dispute is settled — this transition is where oracle-problem judgment calls concentrate
  - Severity assignment and priority assignment as two separately timestamped/attributable transitions, potentially by different actors, so the model doesn't collapse them into one field
- **HUMAN DECISION POINTS**:
  - Deciding a report is a genuine defect vs. intended behavior when no written spec resolves it (the oracle-problem judgment call)
  - Setting/revising severity (impact judgment) as distinct from setting/revising priority (scheduling judgment), and by whom
  - Deciding to close as WorksForMe vs. keep chasing reproduction, weighing reporter credibility and platform-dependence risk
  - Deciding a needinfo has gone stale long enough to resolve Incomplete
  - Deciding WontFix vs. accepting the fix into the queue when severity is acknowledged but cost/benefit says no
  - Triage-owner sign-off on component routing and bug-type classification (defect vs. task vs. enhancement)

## Human gates in a real QA workflow

### Practice

**Entry criteria.** ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-2 (the software-testing-process standard) formally defines entry and exit criteria as the conditions that must hold before a given test sub-process (test design, environment setup, execution, incident reporting) may start or stop, and requires them to be written down in the test plan (ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-2, [iso.org](https://www.iso.org/obp/ui/en/#!iso:std:79428:en); summary at [testing101.net](https://www.testing101.net/post/what-are-the-entry-and-exit-criteria)). In practice this cashes out as a build-acceptance / smoke gate: a build must install, boot, and pass a short automated smoke suite before a QA team accepts it into a test cycle — a pattern reflected in the DoD "Test Readiness Review," which exists to give management assurance that a system has "undergone thorough testing and is ready for turnover to the next test phase" before formal test execution begins ([acqnotes.com](https://www.acqnotes.com/Attachments/Test%20Readiness%20Review.pdf)).

**Exit criteria.** Commonly cited exit conditions across QA guides (BrowserStack, Enov8) are: all planned test cases executed or explicitly deferred, no open critical/high-severity (P1) defects, a final test report with defect metrics produced, and stakeholder sign-off on the results ([BrowserStack](https://www.browserstack.com/guide/entry-and-exit-criteria-in-software-testing), [Enov8](https://www.enov8.com/blog/release-entry-exit-criteria-explained/)). Modern "release readiness dashboard" tooling (TestCollab) operationalizes the same idea as live numbers — pass rate, open-defect count by severity, evidence coverage, execution progress — feeding a GO/NO-GO verdict, explicitly warning that defect counts are the most fragile input because filing/closing discipline in the tracker directly corrupts the dashboard ([testcollab.com](https://testcollab.com/blog/release-readiness-checklist-automated-dashboard)).

**The triage meeting.** Practitioner guides (Shake, ThinkSys, StackLesson) converge on: QA lead facilitates; core attendees are QA lead, dev lead, and product owner/PM, with support/design pulled in as needed; cadence scales with risk — weekly in steady-state, 2–3×/week for active products, daily during a release stabilization window ([Shake](https://www.shakebugs.com/blog/bug-triage-meetings-guide/), [ThinkSys](https://thinksys.com/qa-testing/complete-bug-triage-meeting-process/), [StackLesson](https://www.stacklesson.com/qa-engineering/defect-management/ch05-lesson-05-defect-triage-metrics-and-best-practices/)). The meeting's actual output is decisions, not discussion: assign severity/priority, assign an owner, and make a fix-now/defer/won't-fix call per defect, using a pre-agreed written severity rubric so triage doesn't re-litigate definitions every session (StackLesson).

**Go/no-go and launch readiness reviews.** Google's Launch Coordination Engineering (LCE) checklist, published as Appendix E of the SRE book, structures readiness across nine areas — architecture, capacity, failover, monitoring, security, automation, growth, external dependencies, and rollout schedule — and is deliberately checklist-shaped rather than a single test-pass/fail gate ([sre.google/sre-book/launch-checklist](https://sre.google/sre-book/launch-checklist/)). Notably, Google's own writeup does not prescribe a single sign-off authority or evidence format — it's a curated set of questions LCEs ask, tailored per company/service, not a formal veto process. General go/no-go frameworks (GitScrum, OCM Solution, ProjectSmart) converge on one procedural point regardless of industry: readiness criteria must be agreed *before* the final launch meeting, exceptions require explicit approval with a named owner and a documented risk/follow-up, and go-live must be justified by pointable evidence, not by confidence ([GitScrum](https://docs.gitscrum.com/en/best-practices/launch-readiness-checklist), [OCM Solution](https://www.ocmsolution.com/go-live-checklist/)).

**Sign-off authority and evidence.** In batch-manufacturing-derived QA disciplines (GxP-style "QA disposition"), release authority is formally vested in QA, and any override of a QA hold requires a documented reason code, an e-signature, and an audit trail — the point being that overrides are allowed but never silent ([sgsystemsglobal.com](https://sgsystemsglobal.com/glossary/release-status-hold-release-qa-disposition/)). testRigor's writeup on "QA sign-off" frames it plainly as "the final seal of approval" gating deployment ([testrigor.com](https://testrigor.com/blog/qa-sign-off-the-final-seal-of-approval-explained/)).

### Contested

The sharpest disagreement is whether a *person* should ever hold ship/no-ship veto power at all. The MinimumCD "Beyond CD" practice guide names "QA Sign-Off as a Release Gate" as an explicit anti-pattern: a single QA lead approving releases is a bottleneck and single point of failure, decouples "done" from "deployed," degrades in quality under queue pressure (each release gets a rushed once-over when several are queued), and is structurally incompatible with continuous deployment. Its proposed replacement is to encode the same criteria (test pass rate, security scan, coverage threshold, perf benchmark) into the pipeline itself and let "any engineer trigger a production deployment if the pipeline passes," restricting human review to genuinely novel high-risk changes ([beyond.minimumcd.org](https://beyond.minimumcd.org/docs/anti-patterns/testing/qa-signoff-gate/)).

This directly contradicts the batch/manufacturing-QA lineage and the GxP "QA disposition" model, where a named authority holding hold/release power — with overrides requiring reason codes and audit trails rather than being removed — is treated as the correct control, not a bug ([sgsystemsglobal.com](https://sgsystemsglobal.com/glossary/quality-assurance-qa/)). Neither side is declared the winner in the sources; it tracks a broader unresolved split between regulated/high-consequence domains (where a human-accountable sign-off is load-bearing) and CD-native software teams (where pipeline gates replace it because release volume makes a human gate the throughput ceiling).

A second, quieter disagreement: Google's own LCE checklist is deliberately non-prescriptive about *who* signs off or what *counts* as evidence — each org tailors it — while most commercial "release readiness" tooling and go-live guides insist evidence must be pre-agreed and pointable *before* the meeting. Google's practice tolerates checklist-as-judgment-aid; the checklist-vendor literature treats an un-evidenced "yes" as a failed gate. The sources don't reconcile this; it reflects different tolerances for how much of the gate should be procedural versus expert judgment.

A third tension: "zero known P1" as an exit criterion is treated as standard by classic QA guides (BrowserStack, Enov8) but is silently abandoned in the shift-right/continuous-testing literature, where the whole notion of a pre-release defect census is replaced by staged production exposure (employees → beta → 5% → 100%) as the real filter ([bug0.com](https://bug0.com/knowledge-base/testing-in-devops), Fowler on release toggles, [martinfowler.com](https://martinfowler.com/articles/feature-toggles.html)).

### Implies for the QA cycle

- **Candidate STATES:** `Build Pending Smoke`, `Smoke Passed / Entry-Criteria Met`, `In Test Execution`, `Triage Pending`, `Deferred (Backlog)`, `Fix Verification`, `Exit-Criteria Met / Release-Candidate`, `Readiness Review Pending`, `Go (Approved)`, `No-Go (Blocked)`, `Shipped Under Exception`.
- **Candidate TRANSITIONS:**
  - Build → `Smoke Passed` only on automated smoke-suite pass (evidence: CI smoke run artifact).
  - `Smoke Passed` → `In Test Execution` gated by entry criteria being logged as met (test plan reference, ISO 29119-style).
  - New defect found → `Triage Pending`; triage meeting outcome routes to `Fix Verification`, `Deferred`, or `Won't Fix` (each requires an owner + rationale field).
  - `In Test Execution` → `Exit-Criteria Met` only when: planned cases executed/deferred-with-reason, zero open P1 (or documented exception), test report generated — each a required evidence field, not a checkbox.
  - `Exit-Criteria Met` → `Readiness Review Pending` → `Go`/`No-Go`, where `Go` requires a recorded sign-off identity and `No-Go` requires a recorded blocking reason.
  - Optional escape hatch: `No-Go` → `Shipped Under Exception`, permitted only with a mandatory reason code + named approver + follow-up ticket (mirrors the GxP override pattern) — this transition should be rare, logged, and reportable, not silently equivalent to `Go`.
  - In CD-mode variants: replace the whole `Readiness Review Pending → Go` human step with an automated-gate-pass transition, and reserve the human `Go/No-Go` state only for changes flagged high-risk.
- **Candidate HUMAN DECISION POINTS:**
  - Accepting a build past the smoke gate (can be automated, but someone owns the criteria).
  - Triage meeting: severity assignment, owner assignment, defer/fix/won't-fix call (QA lead + dev lead + PM/PO).
  - Declaring exit criteria met (QA lead attests to evidence, not vibes).
  - Go/No-Go review sign-off (named authority; in some orgs QA holds veto, in CD-native orgs this collapses into pipeline-criteria ownership by the team).
  - Exception approval when shipping over an open No-Go (must be an explicit, escalated decision distinct from normal Go).

## The QA role under automation and AI

### Practice

What CI/regression automation actually absorbed is well documented by the companies that built it at scale. Google's testing team (Mike Bland and colleagues, *Google Testing Blog*, "Flaky Tests at Google and How We Mitigate Them," 2016, testing.googleblog.com) describes an infrastructure where ~1.5% of test runs and ~16% of tests exhibit flakiness at Google's scale, and where regression/smoke gating is a fully automated, always-on function of the CI pipeline — identification, notification, triage, and prevention are treated as discrete pipeline stages, not human judgment calls. Spotify's engineering blog ("Test Flakiness — Methods for Identifying and Dealing with Flaky Tests," engineering.atspotify.com, 2019) describes purpose-built tooling — Odeneye (visualizing flaky clusters) and Flakybot (pre-merge flake detection) — built specifically because flaky CI gating without dedicated tooling degrades trust in the gate itself. Dropbox's sync-engine testing writeup (dropbox.tech, "Testing our new sync engine") describes deliberately serializing async operations onto a single thread specifically to buy determinism back, i.e., flakiness is treated as an architectural property to be engineered out, not just detected.

What practitioners insist automation did *not* absorb is judgment: James Bach and Michael Bolton (DevelopSense, Satisfice.com, various essays including "We. Use. Tools.") draw a hard line between "testing" (evaluating a product through exploration, questioning, modeling, inference) and "checking" (algorithmic comparison of an output to a predetermined expectation), and hold that only checking can be automated — the phrase "there is no such thing as test automation" is a recurring position of theirs. Cem Kaner's writing on "the oracle problem" (kaner.com, "The Oracle Problem and the Teaching of Software Testing") makes the same point from a different angle: an automated check can only report on the specific property it was written to check; a human tester can notice "that's odd" about something the test wasn't looking for at all, because a human carries a model of product intent that the check doesn't.

On the pyramid: Martin Fowler traces the shape's lineage (his 2012 blog post being the most-cited touchstone) to discussions circa 2003–2004 and Jason Huggins' independent 2006 formulation, popularized as doctrine by Mike Cohn's *Succeeding with Agile* (2009). The pyramid's practical claim — many fast/cheap unit tests, fewer integration tests, very few slow/expensive end-to-end/UI tests — became the default architecture for regression suites industry-wide, and Google's own testing team is cited (via Ham Vocke's writeup, referenced in Qase's "test pyramid and its discontents") as explicitly telling teams to "Just Say No to More End-to-End Tests."

On shift-left: the "whole team owns quality" framing holds that quality activities move earlier — into design and coding — and become a property developers are accountable for, not something QA "catches" downstream. Multiple practitioner sources (bugbug.io's shift-left guide; Medium/dev.to practitioner essays) describe this as the stated intent. Where dedicated QA survived, it shifted role: from bug-catcher to test-strategy owner, exploratory specialist, and quality advocate embedded in planning — a repositioning rather than an elimination, per proponents.

On LLM-based test generation specifically: Meta's TestGen-LLM (Alshahwan et al., "Automated Unit Test Improvement Using Large Language Models at Meta," arXiv:2402.09171, FSE 2024) reports concrete, measured numbers rather than hype — on Instagram Reels/Stories, 75% of generated test classes built correctly, 57% passed reliably, and 25% measurably increased coverage; in Meta's internal "test-a-thons" across Instagram/Facebook, 73% of TestGen-LLM's recommendations were accepted into production by human engineers. Crucially, Meta's own design response to hallucination was not to trust the model's judgment but to wrap it in a hard filter pipeline that only accepts LLM output verified to build, pass, and improve coverage over the original — i.e., automation checks the AI's output rather than trusting it. Follow-on work at Meta (Foster et al., "Mutation-Guided LLM-based Test Generation at Meta," arXiv:2501.12862) found that pure coverage-maximizing generation is inadequate for actually killing mutants (a proxy for real bug-catching power), motivating mutation-guided generation as a complement.

### Contested

Whether the pyramid shape is still correct at all is actively disputed. Cucumber's "Eviscerating the Test Automation Pyramid" and Smashing Magazine's 2023 "Long Live the Test Pyramid" argue opposite sides of the same question — critics say the pyramid over-indexes on unit tests that verify units work in isolation but say nothing about whether the system works together, while defenders say the shape is still the right cost/speed trade-off and critics are attacking a strawman version of it. Even the vocabulary is contested: Qase's "test pyramid and its discontents" notes that asking three people to define "unit" in "unit test" reliably produces four different answers, meaning teams arguing about pyramid ratios may not even agree on what the base layer is made of.

Whether shift-left has actually worked is disputed rather than settled. TechRadar Pro ("Shift left might have failed — but AI looks set to deliver on its promise") and a dev.to essay ("Unpopular Opinion: Why Shift Left Testing on Large Complex Systems Is Not Working") argue shift-left underdelivered on large complex systems, with critics noting that teams passing every shift-left gate still ship catastrophic production failures because "the failure modes were in the spaces between their tests, not inside them." Against this, other practitioner writing maintains shift-left succeeded but was frequently *misapplied* — used by management as cover to eliminate dedicated QA headcount rather than to redistribute quality work, per "Dark Patterns: Downsizing QA Teams in the Age of AI" (automationhacks.io) and "Ditching Your QA Team Will Be Your Organisation's Biggest Mistake" (Medium). Neither side disputes that dedicated QA titles are appearing less often on org charts (testgrid.io, "Why the QA Engineer Role Is Disappearing in Modern Teams") — the disagreement is about whether that's a healthy redistribution or a false economy.

On AI agents in testing, the current published evaluations disagree on how far along the technology is. Applitools' "AI Testing in 2026" argues LLMs are reliably strong at test-idea generation, data synthesis, and result summarization, but explicitly states execution and release decisions demand "consistency, repeatability, and explainability" that current agents don't have. Jason Arbon's ongoing evaluation series ("Day 1: Evaluating How Well AI Can Find Bugs," Medium, 2026) ran 15 AI testing agents against a hand-authored benchmark of seeded web-page bugs — a methodologically direct test of the "can it actually find real bugs" claim, with results suggesting wide variance across agents rather than a uniform capability level. Irregular's "Testing AI Agents on Web Security Challenges" reports that without a clear success signal, agents tend to produce false positives and exaggerate the severity of minor findings, struggling to distinguish meaningful signal from noise — a direct empirical instance of the "no severity judgment" critique. This contradicts more optimistic vendor framing elsewhere (acadifysolution.com, bug0.com) that presents AI-driven bug detection as already largely solved for 2026 — the practitioner-vs-vendor gap on this axis is itself an unresolved dispute, not just a nuance.

### Implies for the QA cycle

- **STATES**: `regression-automated` (CI-gated, no human judgment required to pass/fail); `flaky-quarantined` (test isolated from gate pending investigation, time-boxed); `flaky-triaged` (root cause assigned: isolation, nondeterminism, external dependency); `exploratory-chartered` (human-designed session with a mission, not a scripted case); `ai-generated-unverified` (LLM produced a test/bug report, not yet checked); `ai-generated-filtered` (passed automated build/pass/coverage-improvement filter, à la TestGen-LLM); `severity-assigned` (human judgment applied to a finding); `oracle-applied` (a human or model decided what "correct" means for this specific check).
- **TRANSITIONS**: `regression-automated → flaky-quarantined` (on inconsistent pass/fail pattern, e.g. Google's 3-consecutive-failure quarantine trigger); `flaky-quarantined → flaky-triaged → regression-automated` (fix identified, SLA met, e.g. Google's one-sprint SLA) or `→ deleted` (flake deemed unfixable/low-value); `ai-generated-unverified → ai-generated-filtered` (automated gate: builds, passes reliably, improves coverage — never a straight pass-through to production); `ai-generated-filtered → severity-assigned` (still requires human or downstream judgment before acting); `exploratory-chartered → oracle-applied` (finding surfaced requires a human deciding it's actually a bug, not just an automated check firing).
- **HUMAN DECISION POINTS**: deciding what a flaky test's root cause is worth fixing vs. deleting (no automated rule substitutes for this, per Google/Spotify's own tooling design, which surfaces candidates but doesn't auto-resolve); deciding severity of any finding, human- or AI-sourced (explicitly the gap Irregular's security-agent evaluation identifies AI agents as failing at); accepting or rejecting an AI-generated test/bug report into the suite (Meta's 73% human-acceptance rate on TestGen-LLM recommendations is itself evidence this stayed a human gate, not automated); defining the exploratory charter/mission before a testing session (Bach/Bolton's testing-vs-checking line places this irreducibly with a human who holds a model of product intent); deciding whether "shift-left" in a given org means redistributing quality work or eliminating dedicated QA — a staffing/org decision the practitioner literature explicitly says gets abused when treated as automatic rather than deliberate.
